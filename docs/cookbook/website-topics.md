# Website topic map

Reads a site page by page, works out what each page is actually about, and gives
you the map. Useful on your own site — and more useful on somebody else's.

!!! quote "Say this in chat"

    Go through example.com page by page. For each page, tell me the URL, the
    title, and the two or three topics it actually covers. Then give me the
    overall picture: what this site is about, what it covers heavily, and what
    it barely mentions. Save what you learn so I can ask about it later.

**Needs:** web access. Better with the [what it has learned](../memory/wiki.md),
so the results are searchable afterwards.

## Why it works

You cannot hold a forty-page site in your head, and clicking through one page at
a time gives you an impression rather than a map. What each page *says it is
about* and what it *actually covers* are often different, and that gap is where
the useful findings are.

Doing it per page and then summarising is what makes the result trustworthy —
it is built from what is there, rather than from the homepage and an assumption.

## On your own site

**Find what you are unintentionally about.** Sites drift. You will often find
half the pages circling one theme nobody chose.

**Find what is missing.** The thing you consider your main offering, mentioned
once in a footer, is a real and common finding.

**Check it matches how you describe yourself.** If your site's actual topics do
not match your pitch, the site is what prospects read.

## On a competitor's site

This is where it pays. Run it on two or three competitors and you get a
comparison you could not assemble by browsing:

> Do the same for northwind.com, then tell me what they cover that we don't, and
> what we cover that they don't.

That second question is the one worth asking. **What they cover heavily and you
do not** is either a gap in your positioning or a market you deliberately
declined — and it is worth knowing which.

## Content gap analysis

The natural follow-on, and the reason to save the results:

> Based on what you learned about our site and our competitors', what topics
> should we be writing about that we currently aren't?

Because the underlying data is per-page topics rather than a vague impression,
the suggestions are grounded in what is actually published rather than invented.

## Run it as an automation

Mostly this is a one-off you re-run occasionally. But it is worth scheduling on
competitors:

> Re-run the topic map for northwind.com on the first of each month and tell me
> which topics are new since last time.

New topics on a competitor's site are the earliest public signal of where they
are heading — usually visible months before an announcement.

## Worth adjusting

**Bound it.** A large site is a lot of pages. *"Just the blog and the product
pages"* or *"the top 30 pages"* keeps it quick and the result focused.

**Ask for evidence.** *"Quote a line from each page that shows the topic"* makes
the output checkable, which matters if you are about to make a decision on it.
